ABSTRACT

Cloud contamination is a persistent challenge in satellite remote sensing, with approximately 67% of Earth's surface obscured at any given time. While thick clouds are easily detected, thin and cirrus clouds are semi-transparent and frequently missed by conventional detection methods, which rely on static, single-pass classification logic.

This study develops and evaluates a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to enhance thin cloud detection by refining Baseline-generated cloud masks through adaptive, iterative decision-making.

Two RL algorithms—Deep Q-Network (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—were trained to refine the probability maps generated by the s2cloudless baseline. The agents operated on a 20-feature observation space incorporating spectral indicators (Cirrus Band B10, NDSI, Blue/Red Ratio) and learned to perform localized threshold adjustments and thin cloud boosting. The framework was trained on 800 image patches from the CloudSEN12 dataset and evaluated on 200 held-out test images using standard segmentation metrics.

The DQN agent achieved 71.32% thin cloud recall, a 17.60 percentage point improvement over the s2cloudless baseline (53.72%). PPO achieved 62.88% thin cloud recall (+9.17%). DQN also demonstrated 7× greater training efficiency, converging in 100,000 steps compared to PPO's 720,000 steps. Both RL agents improved overall IoU (DQN: 62.44%, PPO: 61.72%) over the baseline (55.93%).

The results demonstrate that Reinforcement Learning provides an effective post-processing approach for recovering thin cloud pixels missed by traditional static classifiers. The DQN algorithm, with its discrete action space, proved superior for cloud mask refinement tasks in terms of both detection accuracy and training efficiency.

Keywords: Cloud Detection, Reinforcement Learning, Deep Q-Network, Proximal Policy Optimization, Sentinel-2, Thin Cloud, Remote Sensing, s2cloudless







CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Background of the Study
Satellite remote sensing has become an essential pillar for global Earth observation, providing critical data for weather forecasting, agricultural monitoring, and climate change research (Gorelick et al., 2017). However, the presence of clouds remains the most significant barrier to the effective use of this imagery, as approximately 67% of the Earth's surface is covered by clouds at any given time (King et al., 2013). While thick, opaque clouds are relatively easy to mask using traditional spectral thresholding, thin and cirrus clouds present a much more complex challenge. These clouds are semi-transparent, allowing surface reflectance to bleed through, which often results in them being misclassified as land or overlooked entirely by standard algorithms (Zhu et al., 2015).

Current industry-standard tools, such as the Fmask (Function of mask) algorithm, s2cloudless (LightGBM ensemble), and various Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), rely heavily on static spectral signatures or local spatial features to identify atmospheric interference. While effective for clear-sky or heavy-cloud scenarios, these "one-shot" classification methods often struggle with the subtle, non-linear patterns of thin clouds, leading to significant errors in downstream applications like NDVI vegetation analysis and sea surface temperature modeling (Li et al., 2019). When thin clouds are under-detected, they introduce atmospheric noise that can lead to false interpretations of environmental data; conversely, over-masking leads to the unnecessary loss of valuable ground information.

To address these nuances, the field of deep learning has recently explored Reinforcement Learning (RL) for complex image processing tasks. Unlike traditional supervised learning, RL frames detection as a sequential decision-making process where an intelligent Agent learns to interact with an Environment to maximize a specific reward (Sutton & Barto, 2018). In the context of computer vision, RL-based agents—specifically those using Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—have shown remarkable success in object localization and fine-grained segmentation by "refining" their focus over multiple iterations (Mathe et al., 2016).

Despite the potential of RL, most current satellite cloud masking frameworks remain limited to static architectures that do not leverage the adaptive, policy-based learning that RL offers. Recognizing this gap, this study applies Reinforcement Learning to the specific problem of thin cloud detection. By framing the detection of semi-transparent pixels as a task for an RL agent, the model can learn a sophisticated policy that balances spectral sensitivity with spatial context. This research aims to enhance the detection of problematic thin clouds, utilizing RL to bridge the accuracy gap left by traditional models. The study evaluates the performance of this RL-integrated approach against baseline methods, specifically measuring its ability to recover spectral integrity in "edge-case" atmospheric conditions.

1.2 Statement of the Problem
The primary challenge in satellite data processing is the persistent failure of automated systems to accurately detect thin or cirrus clouds. While thick, opaque clouds are easily masked, thin clouds are semi-transparent and wispy, making them nearly invisible to standard computer programs. This limitation creates a significant "accuracy gap," where current baseline models fail to detect approximately 46.28% of thin cloud pixels.
This problem stems from three specific technical hurdles:
Low Contrast: Thin clouds blend into the background, lacking the sharp edges needed for standard detection.
Semi-Transparency: The ground is visible through the cloud, causing the computer to confuse land features with atmospheric noise.
Spectral Similarity: Thin clouds are often indistinguishable from haze, fog, or snow when using traditional static algorithms.
Because current Static Machine Learning Classifiers operate on a single-pass, fixed-threshold logic, they struggle with these subtle patterns. This leads to contaminated datasets that skew agricultural monitoring and climate research. This study addresses this gap by framing the detection process as an adaptive, iterative task.

Research Question
To address the limitations of static models, this study seeks to answer the following:
"Can we use Reinforcement Learning to help existing cloud detection systems better identify thin clouds?"

Hypothesis
This research is built upon the following hypothesis: By training an AI agent to specifically look for and boost confidence in thin cloud regions through a trial-and-error reward mechanism, we can significantly improve detection rates without harming the model's performance on thick clouds or clear-sky pixels.


1.3 Objectives of the Study
General Objective
To comparatively evaluate the performance of three cloud-masking approaches for thin and semi-transparent cloud detection in Sentinel-2 imagery: (1) baseline s2cloudless, (2) s2cloudless + DQN refinement, and (3) s2cloudless + PPO refinement.
Specific Objectives
To achieve the general objective, the study aims to:
To develop a hybrid Baseline-RL framework that integrates a s2cloudless baseline  for initial cloud mask generation with a reinforcement learning agent  capable of refining thin cloud regions through iterative patch-wise refinement.
To compare the performance and training efficiency of value-based (DQN) and policy-gradient (PPO) reinforcement learning algorithms for cloud mask refinement.
 To evaluate the proposed framework against conventional Baseline-only methods using standard segmentation metrics—accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score,  and IoU—with emphasis on thin cloud recall improvement.

1.5 Scope and Limitations of the Study
1.5.1 Scope
This study focuses on enhancing the detection of semi-transparent atmospheric features in satellite imagery by implementing a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to bridge the accuracy gap in cloud masking. The primary technical intervention involves the development of an intelligent agent trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Deep Q-Networks (DQN), moving away from traditional static classification toward a sequential decision-making process that learns through trial and error. To establish a rigorous performance baseline, the study compares the proposed RL-based detection against the industry-standard s2cloudless algorithm. This comparison specifically isolates the impact of the RL agent’s reward-based policy on its ability to identify thin and cirrus clouds, which are frequently overlooked by one-shot models.
The imagery is sourced from the Sentinel-2 mission, focusing on multispectral bands to evaluate the model's performance in distinguishing subtle cloud patterns from varying land-cover backgrounds. The study utilizes a diverse dataset of satellite patches containing both thick and thin cloud formations, designed to simulate the challenging atmospheric conditions faced in climate research and agricultural monitoring. The study employs a dual-evaluation protocol: Quantitative statistical performance (Overall Accuracy, F1-Score, and IoU) is evaluated, while Qualitative Visual Analysis is performed on a separate test set to demonstrate the agent's ability to maintain ground-truth integrity.

1.5.2 Limitations
While the proposed RL framework demonstrates improvements in detection accuracy, several constraints define the boundaries of this research. First, the study is strictly delimited to the detection of thin and cirrus clouds; it does not aim to optimize the masking of heavy, opaque cloud structures or cloud shadows, which are already handled sufficiently by existing spectral thresholding methods. Furthermore, the model is trained and validated using Sentinel-2 multispectral data, and its performance on hyperspectral or high-resolution commercial imagery (such as PlanetScope) was not explored in this study.
Additionally, the research does not account for extreme atmospheric anomalies, such as heavy volcanic ash or thick smoke from active wildfires, which may require different spectral band configurations. From a technical standpoint, the RL agents were trained and tested in a controlled desktop environment using the Gymnasium framework; therefore, the study does not evaluate the performance or power efficiency of these models when deployed on edge-computing hardware or real-time on-board satellite systems. Finally, the qualitative analysis is limited to a set of unseen images, providing a snapshot of real-world applicability rather than a global-scale validation across all possible seasonal variations.

	





CHAPTER 2. Review Related Literature

2.1. The Global Challenge of Cloud-Contaminated Satellite Data
In the field of Earth Observation (EO), the presence of clouds is a persistent and multi-billion dollar problem that affects every sector from global commerce to climate safety. Statistical analyses by King et al. (2013) reveal that approximately 67% of the Earth's surface is obscured by cloud cover at any given moment, with certain tropical regions facing near-constant interference. For climate scientists, this lack of visibility creates "blind spots" in critical long-term datasets, making it difficult to monitor sea-surface temperatures or polar ice melt. Without an accurate way to remove these obstructions, the data used for global climate modeling remains fundamentally flawed, leading to potential errors in predicting future environmental shifts (Gorelick et al., 2017).

2.2. Traditional Spectral Thresholding and the Fmask Framework
For decades, the standard approach to solving this problem has been spectral thresholding, most notably through the Function of mask (Fmask). This method relies on the physical reflectance of light; because clouds are typically brighter and colder than the ground, algorithms can be programmed to flag pixels that exceed certain brightness levels. Zhu et al. (2015) successfully expanded this logic to modern missions like Sentinel-2, specifically utilizing the Cirrus band to identify high-altitude clouds. However, this "hard threshold" approach is often too rigid for real-world complexity. In regions where thin clouds overlap with bright urban areas or sandy soil, the spectral signals mix, causing the algorithm to fail—leaving behind the semi-transparent "ghosting" that degrades the quality of the final image.

2.3. Static Classification Methods in Cloud Masking
With the rise of Artificial Intelligence, researchers shifted from simple thresholds to machine learning classifiers, which analyze the "texture" and spatial context of an image rather than just individual pixels. Li et al. (2019) demonstrated that deep learning models could learn to distinguish between the wispy texture of a cloud and the sharp edges of a building. Despite this leap forward, most static classifiers operate on a "one-shot" logic; they look at an image once and make a final classification. This static approach is a key reason why many systems struggle with thin cloud detection. Because these models cannot "re-think" or iteratively process ambiguous pixels, they frequently miss the subtle atmospheric signatures that a more adaptive system might catch.

2.3.1 The s2cloudless Algorithm
	For this study, the s2cloudless algorithm serves as the baseline. It is important to note that s2cloudless is not a Convolutional Neural Network, but rather a LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) ensemble. However, like CNNs, s2cloudless operates as a static classifier: it processes imagery through a fixed decision tree ensemble and applies a global threshold to generate binary cloud masks. This shared characteristic—static, single-pass classification without adaptive refinement—is the fundamental limitation that reinforcement learning aims to address in this study. The RL enhancement framework is therefore applicable to both CNN-based and tree-based static classifiers. Developed by Sinergise for the Sentinel Hub platform, s2cloudless employs a LightGBM gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble trained on 10 of the 13 Sentinel-2 spectral bands (B02, B03, B04, B05, B06, B07, B08, B8A, B11, B12) to generate cloud probability maps (Zupanc, 2017). The algorithm processes input imagery through a series of weak learners that collectively produce a continuous probability value between 0 and 1 for each pixel, which is then thresholded at 0.5 to create the final binary cloud mask. While s2cloudless demonstrates strong performance on thick, opaque clouds, its static threshold approach limits its ability to adapt to semi-transparent atmospheric features—a limitation that provides the motivation for the reinforcement learning refinement approach proposed in this study.

2.4. Reinforcement Learning as a Sequential Decision Process
Unlike standard supervised learning, Reinforcement Learning (RL) frames detection not as a single classification problem, but as a journey of trial and error. As established by Sutton & Barto (2018), RL involves an "Agent" that explores an "Environment," making decisions and receiving rewards based on its success. In the realm of computer vision, this allows for "Active Perception," where the model learns to focus its attention on the most difficult parts of an image. Mathe et al. (2016) found that this sequential approach is particularly powerful for finding objects with low contrast. By allowing an agent to "interact" with the spectral layers of a satellite photo, the model can learn to identify thin clouds through a series of refined guesses, effectively mimicking the way a human expert might squint to see a faint object in the distance.
2.5. Value Estimation through Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
The implementation of reinforcement learning (RL) in high-stakes environments often utilizes Deep Q-Networks (DQN), first introduced by Mnih et al. (2015). This method uses a neural network to estimate the “Quality” (Q-value) of an action—such as deciding whether a pixel is classified as Cloud or Clear. During training, the agent explores different spectral combinations and receives a high reward for every thin cloud it correctly identifies. While early versions of DQN were prone to overestimating Q-values, Mnih et al. (2015) introduced key mechanisms such as experience replay and target networks to stabilize the learning process. These improvements allow the system to learn from past transitions and reduce instability, which is critical when distinguishing thin clouds from visually similar surfaces such as white sand or snow.

2.6. Stability via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
To overcome the "jittery" learning of earlier algorithms, researchers now favor Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Introduced by Schulman et al. (2017), PPO is designed to make training more reliable by ensuring that the agent's "policy" (its strategy) doesn't change too drastically in a single step. This is vital for satellite imagery, where a small change in spectral data can represent a massive change in weather. By using a "clipped" objective function, PPO allows the model to slowly and steadily improve its detection of semi-transparent pixels. This technical stability makes PPO a strong candidate for cloud mask refinement tasks where consistent, incremental improvements are preferred over volatile learning dynamics.

2.7. Hybrid Baselline-RL Architectures for Iterative Refinement
Hybrid architectures that combine static classifiers with Reinforcement Learning represent an emerging paradigm in computer vision tasks requiring both spatial understanding and adaptive decision-making. In this framework, the baseline component performs initial feature extraction and probability estimation, while the RL agent learns to refine these predictions through iterative interaction with the image data.

The key advantage of this hybrid approach lies in its ability to transform static, one-shot predictions into dynamic, context-aware refinements. Wang et al. (2020) demonstrated this principle in medical image segmentation, where an RL agent learned to correct baseline misclassifications through patch-based decision-making. Similarly, GeoAgent (Liu et al., 2023) applied deep reinforcement learning to remote sensing tasks, using an RL agent to adaptively select processing scales during inference.

This study adopts the hybrid paradigm by integrating s2cloudless as the baseline component with two RL algorithms: Deep Q-Network (DQN) for discrete action spaces and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for continuous action spaces. The RL agents observe 64×64 pixel patches and receive a 20-dimensional state vector containing probability statistics, gradient features, and uncertainty ratios. Based on these observations, the agents select actions that adjust the local detection threshold and apply thin cloud boost factors. This patch-wise refinement strategy enables the system to make localized corrections in ambiguous regions while preserving accurate predictions elsewhere.

The reward function guiding the agents is designed to prioritize thin cloud detection:
R = 0.70 × Thin Cloud IoU + 0.30 × F1-Score

This weighted formulation ensures that the agents learn policies specifically optimized for recovering semi-transparent cloud pixels, addressing the primary limitation of static baseline approaches.
The selection of Deep Q-Network (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) as the reinforcement learning algorithms for this study is based on their complementary strengths and suitability for the cloud detection task. DQN is a value-based method that excels in discrete action spaces, making it effective for scenarios where a finite set of threshold adjustments is required. PPO, on the other hand, is a policy-based algorithm known for its stability and efficiency in continuous or high-dimensional action spaces, which is advantageous for fine-grained, adaptive thresholding. Both algorithms are widely adopted, well-supported, and have demonstrated strong performance in image-based reinforcement learning tasks. 

Alternative reinforcement learning algorithms such as Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C), Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG), and Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) were also considered. However, A2C can be less stable and slower to converge in high-dimensional environments, DDPG is primarily suited for continuous action spaces and can be sensitive to hyperparameters, and TRPO, while stable, is more complex to implement and computationally intensive. In contrast, DQN and PPO offer a balance of stability, efficiency, and ease of implementation, making them well-suited for the patch-wise, image-based cloud detection task in this study.
2.8. The CloudSEN12 Benchmark Dataset
For training and evaluation, this study utilizes CloudSEN12, a high-resolution benchmark dataset specifically designed for cloud and cloud-shadow segmentation in Sentinel-2 imagery (Aybar et al., 2022). Unlike binary cloud masks, CloudSEN12 provides pixel-level annotations distinguishing three semantic classes: clear sky (Class 0), thick opaque cloud (Class 1), and thin semi-transparent cloud (Class 2). The study uses 1,000 patches (512×512 pixels), split 80/20 for training and testing.

The dataset includes all 13 Sentinel-2 spectral bands, including the dedicated Cirrus Band (B10) at 1375 nm, which is specifically designed to detect high-altitude ice crystals characteristic of thin cirrus clouds. While the dataset includes all 13 bands, the s2cloudless algorithm utilizes 10 bands (excluding the 60m atmospheric bands B01, B09, and B10), enabling multispectral fusion that combines visible, near-infrared, and shortwave infrared wavelengths.

2.9. Standardizing Learning with the Gymnasium Framework
For an AI to learn through trial and error, it requires a standardized and repeatable "playground." The Gymnasium framework (Towers et al., 2023) has become the global standard for this, providing the interface that connects the AI agent to the raw satellite data. By defining a specific "Observation Space" (the spectral bands) and a "Reward Function" (the goal of the mask), Gymnasium allows researchers to simulate thousands of cloud-masking scenarios in a fraction of the time. This controlled environment provides the agent with the structured feedback loops it needs to recognize thin clouds under diverse conditions. Without such a framework, it would be nearly impossible to provide the agent with the millions of feedback loops required to develop a robust detection policy that works across different continents and seasons.

Chapter 3. Methodology

3.1 Research Design
This study employs a quantitative experimental research design to evaluate the effectiveness of an integrated Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework in enhancing thin cloud detection performance beyond the capabilities of traditional static classifiers.
The study is structured around the manipulation and measurement of the following variables:
Independent Variables (IV):
The Detection Methodology:
Control Group: Static s2cloudless Baseline (Threshold fixed at 0.5).
Experimental Group A: s2cloudless + DQN Agent (Discrete Action Space).
Experimental Group B: s2cloudless + PPO Agent (Continuous Action Space).
Spectral Observation Inputs: Use of Cirrus Band (B10), Blue/Red Ratio, and Normalized Difference Snow Index (NDSI).


Dependent Variables (DV):
Detection Performance Metrics: Recall, Precision, F1-Score, and Intersection over Union (IoU), with emphasis on thin cloud recall improvement over the baseline.
The research procedure is divided into three sequential phases:
Phase 1: Baseline Establishment and Environment Development
In this phase, the s2cloudless is utilized to generate initial cloud probability maps. Simultaneously, a custom Gymnasium environment is developed to translate satellite imagery into a "State Space" where the AI Agent can operate.
Phase 2: Reinforcement Learning Training
The PPO and DQN agents are trained within the custom environment. The agents learn to observe spectral features and Baseline outputs to decide on local threshold adjustments and confidence boosts. Training is governed by a weighted reward function:
Reward=0.70×(Thin_Cloud_IoU)+0.30×(F1_Score)
Phase 3: Performance Evaluation and Comparative Analysis
The final phase involves testing the trained RL agents on an unseen test dataset. A comparative analysis is conducted to determine if the RL-driven dynamic thresholding significantly outperforms the static s2cloudless baseline in "edge-case" thin cloud scenarios.

3.3 System Architecture

Figure 3.3.1 illustrates the architectural design of the Proposed RL-Enhanced Cloud Detection System Pipeline, which operates as a linear, modular workflow. The pipeline begins with the Data Collection and Preparation phase, where Sentinel-2 image patches are acquired from the publicly available CloudSEN12 dataset, then annotated, preprocessed, and organized for analysis. 3.3.1 Baseline Model Selection and Justification
The baseline model used in this study is s2cloudless, a LightGBM-based ensemble model optimized for high-throughput cloud masking of Sentinel-2 data. The algorithm generates a probability map (0–1) for each pixel, which serves as the initial state for the RL refinement agent. The original multispectral band data is preserved to provide the RL agent with raw spectral signatures during decision-making.  The baseline module consists of the following key components:
Probability Mapping: Generates a 0–1 confidence layer that serves as the starting point for the refinement agent.
Feature Preservation: A pathway that maintains the original multispectral band data to ensure the RL agent has access to raw spectral signatures during the decision-making phase.

3.3.2 Reinforcement Learning Refinement (PPO and DQN)
DQN and PPO were selected for their proven effectiveness in discrete and continuous action spaces, respectively, and their successful application in similar image-based RL tasks. For a detailed comparison with alternative algorithms, see Section 2.7.
Following the initial probability generation, the system transitions into the decision-making phase using two distinct Reinforcement Learning architectures: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Deep Q-Network (DQN).
Model Initialization & Training: The agents are trained within a custom Gymnasium environment designed for satellite imagery. The PPO variant is utilized for its stable, continuous policy, allowing for fine-grained threshold adjustments, while the DQN variant is tested for its efficiency in discrete, value-based actions.
Refinement Workflow: The pipeline operates using Patch-Based Logic. High-resolution probability maps and spectral features—specifically the Cirrus Band (B10), Blue/Red Ratio, and NDSI—are processed as 64x64 pixel overlapping patches to maintain local spatial context. For each patch, the RL agent observes the environment and decides whether to lower the threshold (to increase sensitivity) or apply a thin cloud boost to elevate confidence scores.
Comparative Evaluation: The system is designed to facilitate a comparative performance analysis. By keeping the s2cloudless input constant, the study isolates the refinement capability of each RL algorithm, determining which architecture offers the optimal balance of Precision and Recall for thin cloud recovery.

3.4.2 Annotation and Ground Truth Protocol
The ground truth annotations for this study are sourced directly from the CloudSEN12 dataset, which provides pixel-level semantic labels for each 512×512 Sentinel-2 image patch. Each pixel is annotated as one of three classes: clear sky (Class 0), thick opaque cloud (Class 1), or thin semi-transparent cloud (Class 2). These annotations were generated by a team of remote sensing experts using a standardized protocol that combines manual interpretation with automated quality checks, as described in Aybar et al. (2022).

This multi-class annotation scheme enables the RL agent to specifically target thin clouds (Class 2) during training and evaluation. The ground truth masks are used both for supervised validation of baseline models and as the reference for reward calculation in the RL framework. The high-quality, expert-reviewed labels in CloudSEN12 ensure that the evaluation of cloud detection performance is both rigorous and reliable.

3.4.3 Preprocessing and Feature Engineering Pipeline
Following dataset acquisition, a rigorous preprocessing pipeline was established using Python 3.10 and the Rasterio library. This pipeline transforms raw reflectance values into a 20-feature observation space for the agents.
A. Adaptive Image Slicing (Tessellation):
	The CloudSEN12 dataset provides source patches at 512×512 pixels. For localized threshold refinement, each image is subdivided into 64×64 pixel non-overlapping patches, resulting in an 8×8 grid (64 patches per image). This subdivision enables the RL agent to make micro-level decisions that account for regional spectral variations without being overwhelmed by the complexity of the full scene.

The choice of 64×64 pixels is not a universal standard, but it is a commonly used patch size in remote sensing and deep learning studies, especially for tasks such as cloud detection, segmentation, and classification. The selection of patch size is typically a trade-off: smaller patches (e.g., 32×32) provide more localized detail but may miss larger structures, while larger patches (e.g., 128×128) capture more context but can dilute fine details and increase computational cost. The 64×64 patch size is often chosen as a middle ground, offering sufficient spatial context for cloud features while remaining computationally efficient (Aybar et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021).

Many studies, including those utilizing the CloudSEN12 dataset and similar remote sensing benchmarks, have adopted 64×64 or similar patch sizes for patch-based processing (Aybar et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021). The optimal patch size can depend on the dataset resolution, the scale of features of interest, and the model architecture. In this work, 64×64 was selected to balance detection accuracy and resource constraints, as retraining the model with multiple patch sizes (such as 32×32 or 128×128) was not feasible due to computational limitations. However, the literature supports 64×64 as a well-justified and effective choice for this type of task. By operating at this smaller scale, the agent can identify and recover small wisps of cirrus clouds that would otherwise be averaged out in larger processing regions.
B. Spectral Feature Normalization:
To ensure the stability of the PyTorch 2.0 backpropagation, all spectral features were normalized:
Min-Max Scaling: The 10 spectral bands used by s2cloudless were scaled to [0, 1].
Spectral Index Calculation: On-the-fly calculation of the Blue/Red Ratio and NDSI was performed. These indices were concatenated with the baseline's probability output to form the agent's final observation vector.
No-Data Removal: A quality control filter was applied to remove patches with missing sensor data or extreme solar glint, ensuring the agent learns from physically consistent spectral signatures.

3.4.4 Software Stack and Development Environment
The preparation and training phase leveraged a cloud-based Google Colab environment, utilizing a NVIDIA T4 GPU with 16GB of VRAM. This stack enabled persistent storage of the 73 PPO checkpoints and 10 DQN checkpoints via Google Drive integration. The use of Stable-Baselines3 (Raffin et al., 2021) ensured that the data preparation formats were natively compatible with the PPO (continuous) and DQN (discrete) environment wrappers developed in rl_thin_cloud_environment.py.

3.5 Training Procedure
The training phase for the RL-enhanced cloud detection system was conducted using a dual-algorithmic approach to compare the efficacy of continuous versus discrete decision-making. The procedure utilized a custom Gymnasium environment, where the agents learned to act as "intelligent post-processors" for the s2cloudless baseline.
3.5.1 Environment Observation and State Space
Before the training iterations began, the environment was configured to provide the agents with a 20-feature observation vector for every 64x64 pixel patch. This vector serves as the "State" (St) that the agent evaluates before taking an action. The observation includes:
Baseline Probability Stats: Mean, standard deviation, and max/min cloud probability from the s2cloudless baseline.
Spectral Indicators: Normalized NDSI for snow/cloud discrimination, Blue/Red Ratio for scattering analysis, and Cirrus Band (B10) intensity for high-altitude ice detection.
Spatial Context: The normalized row and column position of the patch to account for sensor-specific limb brightening or geographic variations.

3.5.2 Action Space Implementation
The primary objective of the training was for the agent to learn the optimal mapping between the spectral observations and two specific refinement actions:
Threshold Adjustment (ΔT): Modifying the Baseline’s detection cutoff to be more or less sensitive.
Thin Cloud Boost (B): Artificially increasing the confidence score in regions identified by the spectral features as semi-transparent clouds.
As detailed in the experimental setup, PPO explored this as a continuous range, allowing for infinite precision in adjustments, while DQN utilized a discrete set of 15 predefined combinations e.g., Action 0: −0.2 threshold, 0.0 boost.

3.5.3 Reward Function and Optimization Logic
To guide the agents toward maximizing thin cloud detection, a multi-objective reward function was implemented. The system calculates a reward (R_t) for every patch processed:
Thin Cloud IoU: Prioritizes the recovery of pixels labeled as "Class 2" (Thin Clouds) in the CloudSEN12 ground truth.
F1-Score: Acts as a regularizer, ensuring that while the agent "boosts" thin clouds, it does not introduce excessive False Positives (Blue areas in the visualization) that would degrade overall precision.

3.5.4 Iterative Training and Checkpointing
Training was executed on Google Colab using an NVIDIA T4 GPU, following a resumable script architecture to handle session timeouts.
PPO Training: Executed over 720,000 steps (approximately 72 sessions). This high step count was necessary to stabilize the continuous policy gradients and ensure the agent could handle the wide variety of spectral signatures in the 800 training images.
DQN Training: Executed over 100,000 steps (10 sessions). Due to the simpler value-based nature of DQN, the agent reached convergence significantly faster.
Model Persistence: Every 10,000 steps, a model checkpoint was saved to Google Drive using the train_thin_cloud_resumable.py logic. This ensured that the 'Best Model' (highest validation reward) could be selected for the final test set evaluation.


3.6 Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate the effectiveness of the PPO and DQN agents in refining the s2cloudless baseline, a suite of performance metrics was utilized. While standard accuracy is reported, the primary focus of this study is on metrics that capture the model's ability to recover semi-transparent pixels without introducing excessive noise.
3.6.1 Primary Performance Metrics
The following standard metrics were calculated based on the confusion matrix components: True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN).
Precision: Measures the exactness of the model. It defines the proportion of predicted cloud pixels that are actually clouds in the ground truth.


Recall (Sensitivity): Measures the completeness of the model. It defines the proportion of actual clouds in the ground truth that were correctly identified.


F1 Score: Since increasing thin cloud detection (Recall) often leads to a decrease in Precision (false alarms), the F1 Score is utilized as the harmonic mean to provide a balanced evaluation of the system’s performance.



3.6.2 Key Research Metric: Thin Cloud Recall
As the central goal of this research is the recovery of cirrus and semi-transparent clouds, Thin Cloud Recall is designated as the most critical performance indicator. This metric isolates "Class 2" (Thin Clouds) from the CloudSEN12 ground truth to determine exactly how much of the "hidden" atmospheric data was recovered by the RL agent.


This metric directly measures the success of the Thin Cloud Boost action. A higher value here indicates that the agent successfully used the Cirrus Band (B10) and Blue/Red Ratio to identify features the s2cloudless baseline missed.

3.6.3 Intersection over Union (IoU)
Intersection over Union, also known as the Jaccard Index, is used to measure the spatial overlap between the predicted cloud mask and the ground truth. This is particularly important for satellite imagery where the exact shape and boundary of the cloud are vital for downstream geospatial analysis.


During the training phase, the Thin Cloud IoU was given a 70% weight in the reward function, forcing the agent to prioritize high-overlap accuracy for thin cloud formations.

3.6.4 Evaluation Environment
All metrics were computed on the held-out test set of 200 images (20% of the CloudSEN12 subset). By evaluating the PPO and DQN agents on data strictly sequestered from the training and validation loops, the resulting metrics represent a fair and objective measure of the system’s ability to generalize to new real-world satellite scenes.

Multi-Temporal Data Integration: Future studies should explore the use of multi-temporal satellite sequences rather than single-scene patches. Incorporating temporal information could help the RL agent distinguish between persistent surface features and moving atmospheric clouds, potentially further reducing false positives.

Cross-Sensor Generalization: While this research focused on Sentinel-2, the RL framework should be tested on other sensors such as Landsat-8/9 or high-resolution commercial constellations like PlanetScope. This would verify the model's portability across different spectral resolutions and signal-to-noise ratios.

Physics-Informed Reward Functions: It is recommended to integrate atmospheric physics constraints—such as brightness temperature or water vapor absorption models—into the reward function. This could improve the RL agent's stability in complex tropical or polar environments.
Extended Action Space Exploration: Further experimentation with additional discrete actions is suggested, such as "Cloud-Edge Smoothing" or "Shadow Detection," to address minor boundary artifacts occasionally observed in the outputs.

Hybrid PPO-DQN Architectures: Given PPO's fine-grained control and DQN's stability, future work could explore hybrid approaches that leverage the strengths of both algorithms.

5.3.2 Recommendations for Practical Implementation
Edge Computing Optimization: To support real-time disaster response, the DQN model should be optimized for deployment on edge devices using model quantization and pruning techniques. This could enable "on-the-fly" cloud masking before data transmission to ground stations.

Integration with Existing Pipelines: The trained DQN model can be integrated as a post-processing module in existing satellite data pipelines with minimal modification, as it only requires the baseline probability output and spectral bands as inputs.

Threshold Customization: For specific applications requiring different precision-recall trade-offs, the discrete action space can be easily reconfigured to prioritize either higher precision (fewer false positives) or higher recall (fewer missed clouds).
Continuous Model Updates: As new labeled satellite data becomes available, the RL agents can be incrementally retrained to adapt to changing atmospheric conditions or new geographic regions without complete retraining.

5.4 Final Remarks
This research successfully demonstrated that Reinforcement Learning provides an effective and efficient approach for enhancing thin cloud detection in satellite imagery. The DQN agent's ability to achieve a 17.60% improvement in thin cloud recall while requiring 7× fewer training steps than PPO establishes a new paradigm for adaptive cloud masking. By treating cloud detection as a sequential decision-making problem rather than a static classification task, the proposed framework opens new possibilities for intelligent, self-improving Earth observation systems.
