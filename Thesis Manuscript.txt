ABSTRACT


Background: Cloud contamination is a persistent challenge in satellite remote sensing, with approximately 67% of Earth's surface obscured at any given time. While thick clouds are easily detected, thin and cirrus clouds are semi-transparent and frequently missed by conventional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which rely on static, single-pass classification logic.


Objective: This study develops and evaluates a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to enhance thin cloud detection by refining CNN-generated cloud masks through adaptive, iterative decision-making.


Methods: Two RL algorithms—Deep Q-Network (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—were trained to refine the probability maps generated by the s2cloudless CNN baseline. The agents operated on a 20-feature observation space incorporating spectral indicators (Cirrus Band B10, NDSI, Blue/Red Ratio) and learned to perform localized threshold adjustments and thin cloud boosting. The framework was trained on 800 image patches from the CloudSEN12 dataset and evaluated on 200 held-out test images using standard segmentation metrics.


Results: The DQN agent achieved 71.32% thin cloud recall, a 17.60 percentage point improvement over the CNN baseline (53.72%). PPO achieved 62.88% thin cloud recall (+9.17%). DQN also demonstrated 7× greater training efficiency, converging in 100,000 steps compared to PPO's 720,000 steps. Both RL agents improved overall IoU (DQN: 62.44%, PPO: 61.72%) over the baseline (55.93%).


Conclusion: The results demonstrate that Reinforcement Learning provides an effective post-processing approach for recovering thin cloud pixels missed by traditional CNNs. The DQN algorithm, with its discrete action space, proved superior for cloud mask refinement tasks in terms of both detection accuracy and training efficiency.


Keywords: Cloud Detection, Reinforcement Learning, Deep Q-Network, Proximal Policy Optimization, Sentinel-2, Thin Cloud, Remote Sensing, s2cloudless
















CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Background of the Study
Satellite remote sensing has become an essential pillar for global Earth observation, providing critical data for weather forecasting, agricultural monitoring, and climate change research (Gorelick et al., 2017). However, the presence of clouds remains the most significant barrier to the effective use of this imagery, as approximately 67% of the Earth's surface is covered by clouds at any given time (King et al., 2013). While thick, opaque clouds are relatively easy to mask using traditional spectral thresholding, thin and cirrus clouds present a much more complex challenge. These clouds are semi-transparent, allowing surface reflectance to bleed through, which often results in them being misclassified as land or overlooked entirely by standard algorithms (Zhu et al., 2015).
Current industry-standard tools, such as the Fmask (Function of mask) algorithm, s2cloudless (LightGBM ensemble), and various Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), rely heavily on static spectral signatures or local spatial features to identify atmospheric interference. While effective for clear-sky or heavy-cloud scenarios, these "one-shot" classification methods often struggle with the subtle, non-linear patterns of thin clouds, leading to significant errors in downstream applications like NDVI vegetation analysis and sea surface temperature modeling (Li et al., 2019). When thin clouds are under-detected, they introduce atmospheric noise that can lead to false interpretations of environmental data; conversely, over-masking leads to the unnecessary loss of valuable ground information.
To address these nuances, the field of deep learning has recently explored Reinforcement Learning (RL) for complex image processing tasks. Unlike traditional supervised learning, RL frames detection as a sequential decision-making process where an intelligent Agent learns to interact with an Environment to maximize a specific reward (Sutton & Barto, 2018). In the context of computer vision, RL-based agents—specifically those using Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—have shown remarkable success in object localization and fine-grained segmentation by "refining" their focus over multiple iterations (Mathe et al., 2016).
Despite the potential of RL, most current satellite cloud masking frameworks remain limited to static architectures that do not leverage the adaptive, policy-based learning that RL offers. Recognizing this gap, this study proposes a novel approach by applying Reinforcement Learning to the specific problem of thin cloud detection. By framing the detection of semi-transparent pixels as a task for an RL agent, the model can learn a sophisticated policy that balances spectral sensitivity with spatial context. This research aims to enhance the detection of problematic thin clouds, utilizing RL to bridge the accuracy gap left by traditional models. The study evaluates the performance of this RL-integrated approach against baseline methods, specifically measuring its ability to recover spectral integrity in "edge-case" atmospheric conditions.




1.2 Statement of the Problem
The primary challenge in satellite data processing is the persistent failure of automated systems to accurately detect thin or cirrus clouds. While thick, opaque clouds are easily masked, thin clouds are semi-transparent and wispy, making them nearly invisible to standard computer programs. This limitation creates a significant "accuracy gap," where current baseline models fail to detect approximately 46.28% of thin cloud pixels.
This problem stems from three specific technical hurdles:
1. Low Contrast: Thin clouds blend into the background, lacking the sharp edges needed for standard detection.
2. Semi-Transparency: The ground is visible through the cloud, causing the computer to confuse land features with atmospheric noise.
3. Spectral Similarity: Thin clouds are often indistinguishable from haze, fog, or snow when using traditional static algorithms.
Because current Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) operate on a single-pass, static logic, they struggle with these subtle patterns. This leads to contaminated datasets that skew agricultural monitoring and climate research. This study addresses this gap by framing the detection process as an adaptive, iterative task.
Research Question
To address the limitations of static models, this study seeks to answer the following:
"Can we use Reinforcement Learning to help existing cloud detection systems better identify thin clouds?"
Hypothesis
This research is built upon the following hypothesis: By training an AI agent to specifically look for and boost confidence in thin cloud regions through a trial-and-error reward mechanism, we can significantly improve detection rates—targeting the achieved 71.32% thin cloud recall—without harming the model's performance on thick clouds or clear-sky pixels.
1.3 Objectives of the Study
General Objective
The primary goal of this research is to develop and evaluate a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to enhance the detection of thin and semi-transparent clouds in multispectral satellite imagery, bridging the accuracy gap left by traditional static models.
Specific Objectives
To achieve the general objective, the study aims to:
1. To develop a hybrid CNN-RL framework that integrates a CNN baseline  for initial cloud mask generation with a reinforcement learning agent  capable of refining thin cloud regions through iterative pixel-wise decision-making.
2. To compare the performance and training efficiency of value-based (DQN) and policy-gradient (PPO) reinforcement learning algorithms for cloud mask refinement.


3.  To evaluate the proposed framework against conventional CNN-only methods using standard segmentation metrics—accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score,  and IoU—with emphasis on thin cloud recall improvement.

This study focuses on enhancing the detection of semi-transparent atmospheric features in satellite imagery by implementing a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to bridge the accuracy gap in cloud masking. The primary technical intervention involves the development of an intelligent agent trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Deep Q-Networks (DQN), moving away from traditional static classification toward a sequential decision-making process that learns through trial and error. To establish a rigorous performance baseline, the study compares the proposed RL-based detection against industry-standard Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). This comparison specifically isolates the impact of the RL agent’s reward-based policy on its ability to identify thin and cirrus clouds, which are frequently overlooked by one-shot models.

The study employs a dual-evaluation protocol: Quantitative statistical performance (Overall Accuracy, F1-Score, and IoU) is evaluated to measure the jump from the 53.72% baseline to the achieved 71.32% detection rate, while Qualitative Visual Analysis is performed on a separate test set to demonstrate the agent's ability to maintain ground-truth integrity.
1.5.2 Limitations
While the proposed RL framework demonstrates significant improvements in detection accuracy, several constraints define the boundaries of this research. First, the study is strictly delimited to the detection of thin and cirrus clouds; it does not aim to optimize the masking of heavy, opaque cloud structures or cloud shadows, which are already handled sufficiently by existing spectral thresholding methods. Furthermore, the model is trained and validated using Sentinel-2 multispectral data, and its performance on hyperspectral or high-resolution commercial imagery (such as PlanetScope) was not explored in this study.
Additionally, the research does not account for extreme atmospheric anomalies, such as heavy volcanic ash or thick smoke from active wildfires, which may require different spectral band configurations. From a technical standpoint, the RL agents were trained and tested in a controlled desktop environment using the Gymnasium framework; therefore, the study does not evaluate the performance or power efficiency of these models when deployed on edge-computing hardware or real-time on-board satellite systems. Finally, the qualitative analysis is limited to a set of 200 unseen images, providing a snapshot of real-world applicability rather than a global-scale validation across all possible seasonal variations.


        
CHAPTER 2. Review Related Literature


2.1. The Global Challenge of Cloud-Contaminated Satellite Data
In the field of Earth Observation (EO), the presence of clouds is a persistent and multi-billion dollar problem that affects every sector from global commerce to climate safety. Statistical analyses by King et al. (2013) reveal that approximately 67% of the Earth's surface is obscured by cloud cover at any given moment, with certain tropical regions facing near-constant interference. For climate scientists, this lack of visibility creates "blind spots" in critical long-term datasets, making it difficult to monitor sea-surface temperatures or polar ice melt. Without an accurate way to remove these obstructions, the data used for global climate modeling remains fundamentally flawed, leading to potential errors in predicting future environmental shifts (Gorelick et al., 2017).


2.2. Traditional Spectral Thresholding and the Fmask Framework
For decades, the standard approach to solving this problem has been spectral thresholding, most notably through the Function of mask (Fmask). This method relies on the physical reflectance of light; because clouds are typically brighter and colder than the ground, algorithms can be programmed to flag pixels that exceed certain brightness levels. Zhu et al. (2015) successfully expanded this logic to modern missions like Sentinel-2, specifically utilizing the Cirrus band to identify high-altitude clouds. However, this "hard threshold" approach is often too rigid for real-world complexity. In regions where thin clouds overlap with bright urban areas or sandy soil, the spectral signals mix, causing the algorithm to fail—leaving behind the semi-transparent "ghosting" that degrades the quality of the final image.


2.3. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in Cloud Masking
With the rise of Artificial Intelligence, researchers shifted from simple thresholds to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which analyze the "texture" and spatial context of an image rather than just individual pixels. Li et al. (2019) demonstrated that CNNs could learn to distinguish between the wispy texture of a cloud and the sharp edges of a building. Despite this leap forward, CNNs operate on a "one-shot" logic; they look at an image once and make a final classification. This static approach is the primary reason why current systems often stall at a 53.72% recall rate for thin clouds. Because these models cannot "re-think" or iteratively process ambiguous pixels, they frequently miss the subtle atmospheric signatures that a more adaptive system might catch. For this study, the s2cloudless algorithm serves as the CNN baseline. Developed by Sinergise for the Sentinel Hub platform, s2cloudless employs a LightGBM gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble trained on all 13 Sentinel-2 spectral bands to generate cloud probability maps (Zupanc, 2017). The algorithm processes input imagery through a series of weak learners that collectively produce a continuous probability value between 0 and 1 for each pixel, which is then thresholded at 0.5 to create the final binary cloud mask. While s2cloudless demonstrates strong performance on thick, opaque clouds, baseline evaluation on the CloudSEN12 dataset reveals only 53.72% recall for thin cloud pixels—a limitation that stems from the algorithm's inability to adaptively adjust its decision threshold for semi-transparent atmospheric features. This performance gap provides the motivation for the reinforcement learning refinement approach proposed in this study.


2.4. Reinforcement Learning as a Sequential Decision Process
Unlike standard deep learning, Reinforcement Learning (RL) frames detection not as a single math problem, but as a journey of trial and error. As established by Sutton & Barto (2018), RL involves an "Agent" that explores an "Environment," making decisions and receiving rewards based on its success. In the realm of computer vision, this allows for "Active Perception," where the model learns to focus its attention on the most difficult parts of an image. Mathe et al. (2016) found that this sequential approach is particularly powerful for finding objects with low contrast. By allowing an agent to "interact" with the spectral layers of a satellite photo, the model can learn to identify thin clouds through a series of refined guesses, effectively mimicking the way a human expert might squint to see a faint object in the distance.




2.5. Value Estimation through Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
The implementation of reinforcement learning (RL) in high-stakes environments often utilizes Deep Q-Networks (DQN), first introduced by Mnih et al. (2015). This method uses a neural network to estimate the “Quality” (Q-value) of an action—such as deciding whether a pixel is classified as Cloud or Clear. During training, the agent explores different spectral combinations and receives a high reward for every thin cloud it correctly identifies. While early versions of DQN were prone to overestimating Q-values, Mnih et al. (2015) introduced key mechanisms such as experience replay and target networks to stabilize the learning process. These improvements allow the system to learn from past transitions and reduce instability, which is critical when distinguishing thin clouds from visually similar surfaces such as white sand or snow.


2.6. Stability via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
To overcome the "jittery" learning of earlier algorithms, researchers now favor Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Introduced by Schulman et al. (2017), PPO is designed to make training more reliable by ensuring that the agent's "policy" (its strategy) doesn't change too drastically in a single step. This is vital for satellite imagery, where a small change in spectral data can represent a massive change in weather. By using a "clipped" objective function, PPO allows the model to slowly and steadily improve its detection of semi-transparent pixels. This technical stability is what enables the system to bridge the accuracy gap, pushing thin cloud recall from the baseline of 53.72% up to 71.32%.


2.7. Hybrid CNN-RL Architectures for Iterative Refinement
Hybrid architectures that combine Convolutional Neural Networks with Reinforcement Learning represent an emerging paradigm in computer vision tasks requiring both spatial understanding and adaptive decision-making. In this framework, the CNN component performs initial feature extraction and probability estimation, while the RL agent learns to refine these predictions through iterative interaction with the image data.


The key advantage of this hybrid approach lies in its ability to transform static, one-shot predictions into dynamic, context-aware refinements. Wang et al. (2020) demonstrated this principle in medical image segmentation, where an RL agent learned to correct CNN misclassifications through patch-based decision-making. Similarly, GeoAgent (Liu et al., 2023) applied deep reinforcement learning to remote sensing tasks, using an RL agent to adaptively select processing scales during inference.


This study adopts the hybrid paradigm by integrating s2cloudless as the CNN component with two RL algorithms: Deep Q-Network (DQN) for discrete action spaces and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for continuous action spaces. The RL agents observe 64×64 pixel patches and receive a 20-dimensional state vector containing probability statistics, gradient features, and uncertainty ratios. Based on these observations, the agents select actions that adjust the local detection threshold and apply thin cloud boost factors. This patch-wise refinement strategy enables the system to make localized corrections in ambiguous regions while preserving accurate predictions elsewhere.


The reward function guiding the agents is designed to prioritize thin cloud detection:
R = 0.70 × Thin Cloud IoU + 0.30 × F1-Score


This weighted formulation ensures that the agents learn policies specifically optimized for recovering semi-transparent cloud pixels, addressing the primary limitation of CNN-only approaches.


2.8. The CloudSEN12 Benchmark Dataset
The dataset includes all 13 Sentinel-2 spectral bands, including the dedicated Cirrus Band(B10) at 1375 nm, which is specifically designed to detect high-altitude ice crystals characteristic of thin cirrus clouds. While B10 has lower spatial resolution (60m) compared to visible bands (10m), the s2cloudless algorithm leverages all 13 bands through its LightGBM ensemble, enabling multispectral fusion that combines the sharp spatial detail of visible bands with atmospheric sensitivity of infrared bands.
2.9. Standardizing Learning with the Gymnasium Framework
For an AI to learn through trial and error, it requires a standardized and repeatable "playground." The Gymnasium framework (Towers et al., 2023) has become the global standard for this, providing the interface that connects the AI agent to the raw satellite data. By defining a specific "Observation Space" (the spectral bands) and a "Reward Function" (the goal of the mask), Gymnasium allows researchers to simulate thousands of cloud-masking scenarios in a fraction of the time. This controlled environment is what makes the 17.60% improvement possible; it gives the agent the "practice" it needs to recognize thin clouds in the "wild." Without such a framework, it would be nearly impossible to provide the agent with the millions of feedback loops required to develop a robust detection policy that works across different continents and seasons.

Chapter 3. Methodology
3.1 Research Design
This study employs a quantitative experimental research design to evaluate the effectiveness of an integrated Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework in enhancing thin cloud detection performance beyond the capabilities of traditional static Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).
The study is structured around the manipulation and measurement of the following variables:
Independent Variables (IV):
1. The Detection Methodology:
   * Control Group: Static s2cloudless CNN (Threshold fixed at 0.5).
   * Experimental Group A: s2cloudless + DQN Agent (Discrete Action Space).
   * Experimental Group B: s2cloudless + PPO Agent (Continuous Action Space).
2. Spectral Observation Inputs: Use of Cirrus Band (B10), Blue/Red Ratio, and Normalized Difference Snow Index (NDSI).






Dependent Variables (DV):
1. Detection Performance Metrics: Recall, Precision, F1-Score, and Intersection over Union (IoU), specifically targeted at measuring the leap from a 53.72% baseline to a 71.32% thin cloud recall.
The research procedure is divided into three sequential phases:
Phase 1: Baseline Establishment and Environment Development
In this phase, the s2cloudless CNN is utilized to generate initial cloud probability maps. Simultaneously, a custom Gymnasium environment is developed to translate satellite imagery into a "State Space" where the AI Agent can operate.
Phase 2: Reinforcement Learning Training
The PPO and DQN agents are trained within the custom environment. The agents learn to observe spectral features and CNN outputs to decide on local threshold adjustments and confidence boosts. Training is governed by a weighted reward function:
Reward=0.70×(Thin_Cloud_IoU)+0.30×(F1_Score)
Phase 3: Performance Evaluation and Comparative Analysis
The final phase involves testing the trained RL agents on an unseen test dataset. A comparative analysis is conducted to determine if the RL-driven dynamic thresholding significantly outperforms the static CNN baseline in "edge-case" thin cloud scenarios.

Figure 3.3.1 illustrates the architectural design of the Proposed RL-Enhanced Cloud Detection System Pipeline, which operates as a linear, modular workflow designed to process multispectral satellite data through sequential stages of baseline estimation and intelligent refinement. 3.3.1 Pre-trained s2cloudless CNN Baseline
The foundation of the detection architecture is the s2cloudless CNN (Sentinel Hub, 2020), a model specifically optimized for high-throughput cloud masking of Sentinel-2 data. The standard architecture utilizes a gradient-boosted decision tree logic internally to map spectral signatures to probability values where each pixel is assigned a score from 0 to 1.
However, traditional CNN-based masks often rely on a static global threshold (typically 0.5), which can lead to the exclusion of semi-transparent cirrus clouds. To overcome this limitation, the s2cloudless output in this study serves as the "State" for a secondary refinement layer. This allows the architecture to benefit from industry-standard accuracy while focusing computational efforts specifically on "edge-case" thin clouds. The baseline module consists of the following key components:
* Probability Mapping: Generates a 0–1 confidence layer that serves as the starting point for the refinement agent.
* Feature Preservation: A pathway that maintains the original multispectral band data to ensure the RL agent has access to raw spectral signatures during the decision-making phase.


3.3.2 Reinforcement Learning Refinement (PPO and DQN)
Following the initial probability generation, the system transitions into the decision-making phase using two distinct Reinforcement Learning architectures: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Deep Q-Network (DQN).
* Model Initialization & Training: The agents are trained within a custom Gymnasium environment designed for satellite imagery. The PPO variant is utilized for its stable, continuous policy, allowing for fine-grained threshold adjustments, while the DQN variant is tested for its efficiency in discrete, value-based actions.
* Refinement Workflow: The pipeline operates using Patch-Based Logic. High-resolution probability maps and spectral features—specifically the Cirrus Band (B10), Blue/Red Ratio, and NDSI—are processed as 64x64 pixel overlapping patches to maintain local spatial context. For each patch, the RL agent observes the environment and decides whether to lower the threshold (to increase sensitivity) or apply a thin cloud boost to elevate confidence scores.
* Comparative Evaluation: The system is designed to facilitate a comparative performance analysis. By keeping the s2cloudless input constant, the study isolates the refinement capability of each RL algorithm, determining which architecture offers the optimal balance of Precision and Recall for achieving the targeted 71.32% thin cloud recall.


3.4 Data Collection and Preparation
3.4.1 Dataset Source and Generation (CloudSEN12)
While initial exploration utilized manual Sentinel-2 captures, the core of this research utilizes the CloudSEN12 Dataset (Aybar et al., 2022), a state-of-the-art, publicly available repository specifically designed for cloud detection research. CloudSEN12 was selected due to its global diversity and its high-quality, expert-labeled annotations, which are critical for training agents to reach a 71.32% thin cloud recall.
The dataset utilized in this study is characterized by the following parameters:
* Total Volume: 1,000 high-resolution image patches, ensuring sufficient feature density for deep reinforcement learning.
* Spatial Dimensions: Each patch is standardized to 512 × 512 pixels, providing a large enough context for the agent to observe cloud structures while remaining computationally efficient for training in a Google Colab environment.
* Spectral Depth: The dataset includes all 13 Sentinel-2 spectral bands, allowing the system to extract high-altitude ice signals (Band 10) and visible light scattering (Bands 2 and 4).
Data Partitioning Strategy: To ensure rigorous training and unbiased evaluation, the 1,000-image collection was organized into two strictly separated datasets:
1. Model Development Dataset (800 Images): This primary set was utilized for the iterative training loops of the PPO and DQN agents. It was further partitioned at the patch level into a Training Set (80%) for gradient updates and a Validation Set (20%) to monitor for overfitting.
2. Held-Out Test Dataset (200 Images): These images were strictly sequestered from the development phase. They serve as the definitive benchmark for the results presented in Chapter 4, ensuring that the model's performance reflects true generalization to unseen atmospheric conditions.


3.4.2 Annotation and Ground Truth Protocol
Unlike binary cloud masks, the CloudSEN12 ground truth utilizes a multi-class semantic labeling system. This allows the RL agent to specifically target "Class 2" (Thin Clouds/Cirrus) during the reward calculation phase.


3.4.3 Preprocessing and Feature Engineering Pipeline
Following dataset acquisition, a rigorous preprocessing pipeline was established using Python 3.10 and the Rasterio library. This pipeline transforms raw reflectance values into a 20-feature observation space for the agents.
A. Adaptive Image Slicing (Tessellation):
        The CloudSEN12 dataset provides source patches at 512×512 pixels. For localized threshold refinement, each image is subdivided into 64×64 pixel non-overlapping patches, resulting in an 8×8 grid (64 patches per image). This subdivision enables the RL agent to make micro-level decisions that account for regional spectral variations without being overwhelmed by the complexity of the full scene. By operating at this smaller scale, the agent can identify and recover small wisps of cirrus clouds that would otherwise be averaged out in larger processing regions.
B. Spectral Feature Normalization:
To ensure the stability of the PyTorch 2.0 backpropagation, all spectral features were normalized:
1. Min-Max Scaling: All 13 bands were scaled to [0, 1].
2. Spectral Index Calculation: On-the-fly calculation of the Blue/Red Ratio and NDSI was performed. These indices were concatenated with the CNN’s probability output to form the agent’s final observation vector.
3. No-Data Removal: A quality control filter was applied to remove patches with missing sensor data or extreme solar glint, ensuring the agent learns from physically consistent spectral signatures.


3.4.4 Software Stack and Development Environment
The preparation and training phase leveraged a cloud-based Google Colab environment, utilizing a NVIDIA T4 GPU with 16GB of VRAM. This stack enabled persistent storage of the 73 PPO checkpoints and 10 DQN checkpoints via Google Drive integration. The use of Stable-Baselines3 (Raffin et al., 2021) ensured that the data preparation formats were natively compatible with the PPO (continuous) and DQN (discrete) environment wrappers developed in rl_thin_cloud_environment.py.


3.5 Training Procedure
The training phase for the RL-enhanced cloud detection system was conducted using a dual-algorithmic approach to compare the efficacy of continuous versus discrete decision-making. The procedure utilized a custom Gymnasium environment, where the agents learned to act as "intelligent post-processors" for the s2cloudless baseline.
3.5.1 Environment Observation and State Space
Before the training iterations began, the environment was configured to provide the agents with a 20-feature observation vector for every 64x64 pixel patch. This vector serves as the "State" (St) that the agent evaluates before taking an action. The observation includes:
* CNN Probability Stats: Mean, standard deviation, and max/min cloud probability from the s2cloudless baseline.
* Spectral Indicators: Normalized NDSI for snow/cloud discrimination, Blue/Red Ratio for scattering analysis, and Cirrus Band (B10) intensity for high-altitude ice detection.
* Spatial Context: The normalized row and column position of the patch to account for sensor-specific limb brightening or geographic variations.


3.5.2 Action Space Implementation
The primary objective of the training was for the agent to learn the optimal mapping between the spectral observations and two specific refinement actions:
1. Threshold Adjustment (ΔT): Modifying the CNN’s detection cutoff to be more or less sensitive.
2. Thin Cloud Boost (B): Artificially increasing the confidence score in regions identified by the spectral features as semi-transparent clouds.
As detailed in the experimental setup, PPO explored this as a continuous range, allowing for infinite precision in adjustments, while DQN utilized a discrete set of 15 predefined combinations e.g., Action 0: −0.2 threshold, 0.0 boost.


3.5.3 Reward Function and Optimization Logic
To guide the agents toward the 71.32% Thin Cloud Recall goal, a multi-objective reward function was implemented. The system calculates a reward (Rt) for every patch processed:  
* Thin Cloud IoU: Prioritizes the recovery of pixels labeled as "Class 2" (Thin Clouds) in the CloudSEN12 ground truth.
* F1-Score: Acts as a regularizer, ensuring that while the agent "boosts" thin clouds, it does not introduce excessive False Positives (Blue areas in the visualization) that would degrade overall precision.


3.5.4 Iterative Training and Checkpointing
Training was executed on Google Colab using an NVIDIA T4 GPU, following a resumable script architecture to handle session timeouts.
* PPO Training: Executed over 720,000 steps (approximately 72 sessions). This high step count was necessary to stabilize the continuous policy gradients and ensure the agent could handle the wide variety of spectral signatures in the 800 training images.
* DQN Training: Executed over 100,000 steps (10 sessions). Due to the simpler value-based nature of DQN, the agent reached convergence significantly faster.
* Model Persistence: Every 10,000 steps, a model checkpoint was saved to Google Drive using the train_thin_cloud_resumable.py logic. This ensured that the 'Best Model' (highest validation mAP) could be selected for the final test set evaluation.




3.6 Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate the effectiveness of the PPO and DQN agents in refining the s2cloudless baseline, a suite of performance metrics was utilized. While standard accuracy is reported, the primary focus of this study is on metrics that capture the model's ability to recover semi-transparent pixels without introducing excessive noise.
3.6.1 Primary Performance Metrics
The following standard metrics were calculated based on the confusion matrix components: True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN).
* Precision: Measures the exactness of the model. It defines the proportion of predicted cloud pixels that are actually clouds in the ground truth.  




   * Recall (Sensitivity): Measures the completeness of the model. It defines the proportion of actual clouds in the ground truth that were correctly identified.
  



   * F1 Score: Since increasing thin cloud detection (Recall) often leads to a decrease in Precision (false alarms), the F1 Score is utilized as the harmonic mean to provide a balanced evaluation of the system’s performance.  






3.6.2 Key Research Metric: Thin Cloud Recall
As the central goal of this research is the recovery of cirrus and semi-transparent clouds, Thin Cloud Recall is designated as the most critical performance indicator. This metric isolates "Class 2" (Thin Clouds) from the CloudSEN12 ground truth to determine exactly how much of the "hidden" atmospheric data was recovered by the RL agent.
  



This metric directly measures the success of the Thin Cloud Boost action. A higher value here indicates that the agent successfully used the Cirrus Band (B10) and Blue/Red Ratio to identify features the CNN baseline missed.
3.6.3 Intersection over Union (IoU)
Intersection over Union, also known as the Jaccard Index, is used to measure the spatial overlap between the predicted cloud mask and the ground truth. This is particularly important for satellite imagery where the exact shape and boundary of the cloud are vital for downstream geospatial analysis.  




During the training phase, the Thin Cloud IoU was given a 70% weight in the reward function, forcing the agent to prioritize high-overlap accuracy for thin cloud formations.


3.6.4 Evaluation Environment
All metrics were computed on the held-out test set of 200 images (20% of the CloudSEN12 subset). By evaluating the PPO and DQN agents on data strictly sequestered from the training and validation loops, the resulting metrics represent a fair and objective measure of the system’s ability to generalize to new real-world satellite scenes.

4.1.3 Discussion: CNN Limitations and Conservative Bias
The Phase 1 results confirm that the s2cloudless baseline operates with a static global threshold (0.5), which creates systematic under-detection in regions with semi-transparent clouds. Analysis of the misclassified pixels revealed three primary failure modes:

      2. Mixed Pixels: Boundary regions where thin clouds partially overlap with ground features produced probability values below the detection threshold.
      3. High-Altitude Ice Crystals: Despite the availability of Cirrus Band data, the baseline's RGB-weighted architecture failed to leverage this spectral information effectively.


These findings validated the need for an adaptive refinement layer capable of locally adjusting detection sensitivity based on spectral context—precisely the capability that reinforcement learning provides.

The training efficiency difference can be attributed to three key factors:
      1. Action Space Complexity: PPO must learn a probability distribution over an infinite continuous range, while DQN only needs to estimate Q-values for 15 discrete options.

This finding has practical implications for deployment: DQN's faster training enables quicker iteration and adaptation to new geographic regions or sensor configurations.

CHAPTER 5. Conclusion and Recommendation
This chapter synthesizes the findings from the three-phase experimental evaluation and provides conclusions and recommendations for future research and practical implementation.


5.1 Summary of Findings by Phase
5.1.1 Phase 1: Baseline Establishment and Environment Development
The first phase successfully established the foundation for the RL-enhanced cloud detection system:
      * CNN Baseline Performance: The s2cloudless CNN baseline achieved an overall accuracy of 78.50% but exhibited a critical limitation in thin cloud detection, with only 53.72% thin cloud recall. This confirmed the "conservative bias" identified in the problem statement.


      * Environment Development: A custom Gymnasium environment was successfully developed, translating satellite spectral data into a 20-feature observation space. The integration of Cirrus Band (B10), NDSI, and Blue/Red Ratio features proved essential for enabling the RL agents to detect semi-transparent atmospheric features.
      * Data Pipeline: The CloudSEN12 dataset (1,000 patches) was successfully processed and partitioned into training (800 patches) and test (200 patches) sets, providing sufficient diversity for robust model development.


5.1.2 Phase 2: Reinforcement Learning Training
The second phase demonstrated the feasibility and efficiency of RL-based cloud mask refinement:
      * PPO Training: The Proximal Policy Optimization agent required 720,000 training steps (~72 sessions) to achieve stable convergence. The continuous action space allowed fine-grained threshold adjustments but resulted in longer training times and occasional mask artifacts.


      * DQN Training: The Deep Q-Network agent achieved convergence in only 100,000 steps (~10 sessions)—7× faster than PPO. The discrete action space (15 predefined threshold-boost combinations) proved more suitable for the threshold refinement task.


      * Training Efficiency: The significant difference in training requirements revealed that value-based RL with discrete actions is more sample-efficient than policy-gradient methods for pixel-wise classification refinement tasks.


5.1.3 Phase 3: Performance Evaluation and Comparative Analysis
The final phase validated the effectiveness of the proposed RL framework:
      * Quantitative Results: The DQN agent achieved the best thin cloud detection with 71.32% thin cloud recall (+17.60% improvement), 76.88% F1-score, and 62.44% IoU. PPO achieved 80.49% accuracy and 62.88% thin cloud recall (+9.17% improvement).


      * Qualitative Analysis: Visual inspection confirmed that DQN produced cleaner mask boundaries with fewer artifacts compared to PPO, while both RL agents significantly outperformed the CNN baseline in recovering semi-transparent cloud formations.


      * Precision-Recall Balance: The DQN achieved substantial recall improvements while only sacrificing 15.00% precision, demonstrating that the RL refinement approach does not introduce excessive false positives.




5.2 Conclusions
Based on the three-phase experimental evaluation, the following conclusions are drawn:
Conclusion 1: RL Post-Processing Successfully Addresses CNN Limitations
The integration of a Reinforcement Learning refinement layer proved to be a highly effective post-processing method for enhancing cloud mask quality. By treating threshold adjustment as a sequential decision-making process, both PPO and DQN agents successfully recovered thin cloud pixels that the static CNN baseline missed. This validates the central hypothesis that RL can bridge the accuracy gap left by traditional detection methods.


Conclusion 2: DQN Outperforms PPO for Threshold Refinement Tasks
The Deep Q-Network emerged as the superior algorithm for thin cloud detection, achieving:
      * Better thin cloud recall: 71.32% vs. 62.88% (+8.44 percentage points)
      * Higher IoU: 62.44% vs. 61.72%
      * Greater efficiency: 100,000 steps vs. 720,000 steps (7× faster)
      * Cleaner outputs: More contiguous mask boundaries with fewer artifacts
While PPO achieved slightly higher overall accuracy (80.49% vs. 78.37%), DQN's superior performance on the primary research metric—thin cloud recall—makes it the preferred choice for this application.


Conclusion 3: Spectral Feature Integration Enables "Invisible" Cloud Detection
The RL agents successfully leveraged spectral indicators—particularly the Cirrus Band (B10), NDSI, and Blue/Red Ratio—to identify high-altitude ice crystals and semi-transparent formations that were physically present in the multispectral data but undetectable by standard RGB-based architectures. This confirms the importance of spectral feature engineering in the observation space design.

5.3 Recommendations
Based on the findings and limitations observed during this study, the following recommendations are proposed for future research and practical implementation:
5.3.1 Recommendations for Future Research
      1. Multi-Temporal Data Integration: Future studies should explore the use of multi-temporal satellite sequences rather than single-scene patches. Incorporating temporal information could help the RL agent distinguish between persistent surface features and moving atmospheric clouds, potentially further reducing false positives.


      2. Cross-Sensor Generalization: While this research focused on Sentinel-2, the RL framework should be tested on other sensors such as Landsat-8/9 or high-resolution commercial constellations like PlanetScope. This would verify the model's portability across different spectral resolutions and signal-to-noise ratios.


      3. Physics-Informed Reward Functions: It is recommended to integrate atmospheric physics constraints—such as brightness temperature or water vapor absorption models—into the reward function. This could improve the RL agent's stability in complex tropical or polar environments.


      4. Extended Action Space Exploration: Further experimentation with additional discrete actions is suggested, such as "Cloud-Edge Smoothing" or "Shadow Detection," to address minor boundary artifacts occasionally observed in the outputs.


      5. Hybrid PPO-DQN Architectures: Given PPO's fine-grained control and DQN's stability, future work could explore hybrid approaches that leverage the strengths of both algorithms.


5.3.2 Recommendations for Practical Implementation
      1. Edge Computing Optimization: To support real-time disaster response, the DQN model should be optimized for deployment on edge devices using model quantization and pruning techniques. This could enable "on-the-fly" cloud masking before data transmission to ground stations.


      2. Integration with Existing Pipelines: The trained DQN model can be integrated as a post-processing module in existing satellite data pipelines with minimal modification, as it only requires the CNN probability output and spectral bands as inputs.


      3. Threshold Customization: For specific applications requiring different precision-recall trade-offs, the discrete action space can be easily reconfigured to prioritize either higher precision (fewer false positives) or higher recall (fewer missed clouds).
      4. Continuous Model Updates: As new labeled satellite data becomes available, the RL agents can be incrementally retrained to adapt to changing atmospheric conditions or new geographic regions without complete retraining.


5.4 Final Remarks
This research successfully demonstrated that Reinforcement Learning provides an effective and efficient approach for enhancing thin cloud detection in satellite imagery. The DQN agent's ability to achieve a 17.60% improvement in thin cloud recall while requiring 7× fewer training steps than PPO establishes a new paradigm for adaptive cloud masking. By treating cloud detection as a sequential decision-making problem rather than a static classification task, the proposed framework opens new possibilities for intelligent, self-improving Earth observation systems.
