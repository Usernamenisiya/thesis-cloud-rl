CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Background of the Study
Satellite remote sensing has become an essential pillar for global Earth observation, providing critical data for weather forecasting, agricultural monitoring, and climate change research (Gorelick et al., 2017). However, the presence of clouds remains the most significant barrier to the effective use of this imagery, as approximately 67% of the Earth's surface is covered by clouds at any given time (King et al., 2013). While thick, opaque clouds are relatively easy to mask using traditional spectral thresholding, thin and cirrus clouds present a much more complex challenge. These clouds are semi-transparent, allowing surface reflectance to bleed through, which often results in them being misclassified as land or overlooked entirely by standard algorithms (Zhu et al., 2015).
Current industry-standard tools, such as the Fmask (Function of mask) algorithm and various Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), rely heavily on static spectral signatures or local spatial features to identify atmospheric interference. While effective for clear-sky or heavy-cloud scenarios, these "one-shot" classification methods often struggle with the subtle, non-linear patterns of thin clouds, leading to significant errors in downstream applications like NDVI vegetation analysis and sea surface temperature modeling (Li et al., 2019). When thin clouds are under-detected, they introduce atmospheric noise that can lead to false interpretations of environmental data; conversely, over-masking leads to the unnecessary loss of valuable ground information.
To address these nuances, the field of deep learning has recently explored Reinforcement Learning (RL) for complex image processing tasks. Unlike traditional supervised learning, RL frames detection as a sequential decision-making process where an intelligent Agent learns to interact with an Environment to maximize a specific reward (Sutton & Barto, 2018). In the context of computer vision, RL-based agents—specifically those using Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—have shown remarkable success in object localization and fine-grained segmentation by "refining" their focus over multiple iterations (Mathe et al., 2016).
Despite the potential of RL, most current satellite cloud masking frameworks remain limited to static architectures that do not leverage the adaptive, policy-based learning that RL offers. Recognizing this gap, this study proposes a novel approach by applying Reinforcement Learning to the specific problem of thin cloud detection. By framing the detection of semi-transparent pixels as a task for an RL agent, the model can learn a sophisticated policy that balances spectral sensitivity with spatial context. This research aims to enhance the detection of problematic thin clouds, utilizing RL to bridge the accuracy gap left by traditional models. The study evaluates the performance of this RL-integrated approach against baseline methods, specifically measuring its ability to recover spectral integrity in "edge-case" atmospheric conditions.


1.2 Statement of the Problem
The primary challenge in satellite data processing is the persistent failure of automated systems to accurately detect thin or cirrus clouds. While thick, opaque clouds are easily masked, thin clouds are semi-transparent and wispy, making them nearly invisible to standard computer programs. This limitation creates a significant "accuracy gap," where current baseline models miss approximately 36.72% of thin cloud interference.
This problem stems from three specific technical hurdles:
1. Low Contrast: Thin clouds blend into the background, lacking the sharp edges needed for standard detection.
2. Semi-Transparency: The ground is visible through the cloud, causing the computer to confuse land features with atmospheric noise.
3. Spectral Similarity: Thin clouds are often indistinguishable from haze, fog, or snow when using traditional static algorithms.
Because current Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) operate on a single-pass, static logic, they struggle with these subtle patterns. This leads to contaminated datasets that skew agricultural monitoring and climate research. This study addresses this gap by framing the detection process as an adaptive, iterative task.
Research Question
To address the limitations of static models, this study seeks to answer the following:
"Can we use Reinforcement Learning to help existing cloud detection systems better identify thin clouds?"
Hypothesis
This research is built upon the following hypothesis: By training an AI agent to specifically look for and boost confidence in thin cloud regions through a trial-and-error reward mechanism, we can significantly improve detection rates—targeting the achieved 77.00% accuracy—without harming the model's performance on thick clouds or clear-sky pixels.
1.3 Objectives of the Study
General Objective
The primary goal of this research is to develop and evaluate a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to enhance the detection of thin and semi-transparent clouds in multispectral satellite imagery, bridging the accuracy gap left by traditional static models.
Specific Objectives
To achieve the general objective, the study aims to:
1. Develop an RL Environment: Create a custom Gymnasium-based environment that translates satellite spectral bands into an "observation space" for a Reinforcement Learning agent.
2. Implement Agent Architectures: Train and fine-tune Reinforcement Learning agents using Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to learn optimal policies for cloud pixel identification.
3. Optimize for Thin Clouds: Design a specific reward function that penalizes false negatives in semi-transparent regions, forcing the agent to prioritize the detection of elusive cirrus clouds.
4. Evaluate Performance: Compare the RL-based approach against standard baseline models (CNNs and thresholding) using key metrics, specifically targeting an improvement in thin cloud detection from 63.28% to at least 77.00%.
5. Assess Stability: Analyze the overall accuracy and F1-score to ensure that the increased sensitivity to thin clouds does not result in higher false-positive rates for clear-sky or thick-cloud regions.

1.5 Scope and Limitations of the Study
1.5.1 Scope
This study focuses on enhancing the detection of semi-transparent atmospheric features in satellite imagery by implementing a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to bridge the accuracy gap in cloud masking. The primary technical intervention involves the development of an intelligent agent trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Deep Q-Networks (DQN), moving away from traditional static classification toward a sequential decision-making process that learns through trial and error. To establish a rigorous performance baseline, the study compares the proposed RL-based detection against industry-standard Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). This comparison specifically isolates the impact of the RL agent’s reward-based policy on its ability to identify thin and cirrus clouds, which are frequently overlooked by one-shot models.
The imagery is sourced from the Sentinel-2 mission, focusing on multispectral bands to evaluate the model's performance in distinguishing subtle cloud patterns from varying land-cover backgrounds. The study utilizes a diverse dataset of satellite patches containing both thick and thin cloud formations, designed to simulate the challenging atmospheric conditions faced in climate research and agricultural monitoring. The study employs a dual-evaluation protocol: Quantitative statistical performance (Overall Accuracy, F1-Score, and IoU) is evaluated to measure the jump from the 63.28% baseline to the achieved 77.00% detection rate, while Qualitative Visual Analysis is performed on a separate test set to demonstrate the agent's ability to maintain ground-truth integrity.
1.5.2 Limitations
While the proposed RL framework demonstrates significant improvements in detection accuracy, several constraints define the boundaries of this research. First, the study is strictly delimited to the detection of thin and cirrus clouds; it does not aim to optimize the masking of heavy, opaque cloud structures or cloud shadows, which are already handled sufficiently by existing spectral thresholding methods. Furthermore, the model is trained and validated using Sentinel-2 multispectral data, and its performance on hyperspectral or high-resolution commercial imagery (such as PlanetScope) was not explored in this study.
Additionally, the research does not account for extreme atmospheric anomalies, such as heavy volcanic ash or thick smoke from active wildfires, which may require different spectral band configurations. From a technical standpoint, the RL agents were trained and tested in a controlled desktop environment using the Gymnasium framework; therefore, the study does not evaluate the performance or power efficiency of these models when deployed on edge-computing hardware or real-time on-board satellite systems. Finally, the qualitative analysis is limited to a set of 100 unseen images, providing a snapshot of real-world applicability rather than a global-scale validation across all possible seasonal variations.


        






















CHAPTER 2. Review Related Literature
2.1. The Global Challenge of Cloud-Contaminated Satellite Data
In the field of Earth Observation (EO), the presence of clouds is a persistent and multi-billion dollar problem that affects every sector from global commerce to climate safety. Statistical analyses by King et al. (2013) reveal that approximately 67% of the Earth's surface is obscured by cloud cover at any given moment, with certain tropical regions facing near-constant interference. For climate scientists, this lack of visibility creates "blind spots" in critical long-term datasets, making it difficult to monitor sea-surface temperatures or polar ice melt. Without an accurate way to remove these obstructions, the data used for global climate modeling remains fundamentally flawed, leading to potential errors in predicting future environmental shifts (Gorelick et al., 2017).
2.2. Traditional Spectral Thresholding and the Fmask Framework
For decades, the standard approach to solving this problem has been spectral thresholding, most notably through the Function of mask (Fmask). This method relies on the physical reflectance of light; because clouds are typically brighter and colder than the ground, algorithms can be programmed to flag pixels that exceed certain brightness levels. Zhu et al. (2015) successfully expanded this logic to modern missions like Sentinel-2, specifically utilizing the Cirrus band to identify high-altitude clouds. However, this "hard threshold" approach is often too rigid for real-world complexity. In regions where thin clouds overlap with bright urban areas or sandy soil, the spectral signals mix, causing the algorithm to fail—leaving behind the semi-transparent "ghosting" that degrades the quality of the final image.
2.3. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in Cloud Masking
With the rise of Artificial Intelligence, researchers shifted from simple thresholds to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which analyze the "texture" and spatial context of an image rather than just individual pixels. Li et al. (2019) demonstrated that CNNs could learn to distinguish between the wispy texture of a cloud and the sharp edges of a building. Despite this leap forward, CNNs operate on a "one-shot" logic; they look at an image once and make a final classification. This static approach is the primary reason why current systems often stall at a 63.28% accuracy rate for thin clouds. Because these models cannot "re-think" or iteratively process ambiguous pixels, they frequently miss the subtle atmospheric signatures that a more adaptive system might catch.
2.4. Reinforcement Learning as a Sequential Decision Process
Unlike standard deep learning, Reinforcement Learning (RL) frames detection not as a single math problem, but as a journey of trial and error. As established by Sutton & Barto (2018), RL involves an "Agent" that explores an "Environment," making decisions and receiving rewards based on its success. In the realm of computer vision, this allows for "Active Perception," where the model learns to focus its attention on the most difficult parts of an image. Mathe et al. (2016) found that this sequential approach is particularly powerful for finding objects with low contrast. By allowing an agent to "interact" with the spectral layers of a satellite photo, the model can learn to identify thin clouds through a series of refined guesses, effectively mimicking the way a human expert might squint to see a faint object in the distance.
2.5. Value Estimation through Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
The implementation of RL in high-stakes environments often utilizes Deep Q-Networks (DQN). This method uses a neural network to estimate the "Quality" (Q-value) of an action—such as deciding whether a pixel is "Cloud" or "Clear." During training, the agent explores different spectral combinations and receives a high reward for every thin cloud it correctly identifies. While early versions of DQN were prone to overestimating these rewards, modern variants have introduced "Experience Replay" and "Target Networks" to stabilize the learning process. This allows the system to remember past mistakes and avoid them in the future, which is critical when trying to distinguish between a thin, white cloud and a patch of white sand or snow on the ground.
2.6. Stability via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
To overcome the "jittery" learning of earlier algorithms, researchers now favor Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Introduced by Schulman et al. (2017), PPO is designed to make training more reliable by ensuring that the agent's "policy" (its strategy) doesn't change too drastically in a single step. This is vital for satellite imagery, where a small change in spectral data can represent a massive change in weather. By using a "clipped" objective function, PPO allows the model to slowly and steadily improve its detection of semi-transparent pixels. This technical stability is what enables the system to bridge the accuracy gap, pushing thin cloud detection from the baseline of 63.28% up to the targeted 77.00%.
2.7. The Importance of the Cirrus Band and Multispectral Fusion
The Sentinel-2 mission includes a dedicated Cirrus Band (Band 10) designed specifically to catch high-altitude, thin clouds that other sensors might miss. However, a major hurdle in remote sensing is that this band often has a lower resolution than the standard RGB bands. Recent literature suggests that the key to perfect cloud masking lies in "Multispectral Fusion"—combining the sharp detail of the visible bands with the atmospheric sensitivity of the infrared bands. An RL agent is uniquely suited for this task because it can learn an adaptive weighting strategy, deciding for itself which bands are the most "trustworthy" based on the specific location or time of day the image was captured.
2.8. Architectural Refinement: The Role of Activation Functions
The "brain" of any AI model is its activation function, which determines how it processes complex patterns. While many models use PReLU, recent advancements have highlighted the Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU), or Swish. Research indicates that SiLU provides a smoother "gradient flow," meaning the model can learn subtle features more efficiently without the mathematical errors common in standard ReLU functions. This is particularly relevant for thin cloud detection; because the difference between a thin cloud and a clear sky is so small, the model needs a highly sensitive activation function to capture these non-linear patterns. Utilizing SiLU in the RL agent's backbone can significantly improve training stability in these high-stakes visual environments.
2.9. Standardizing Learning with the Gymnasium Framework
For an AI to learn through trial and error, it requires a standardized and repeatable "playground." The Gymnasium framework (Towers et al., 2023) has become the global standard for this, providing the interface that connects the AI agent to the raw satellite data. By defining a specific "Observation Space" (the spectral bands) and a "Reward Function" (the goal of the mask), Gymnasium allows researchers to simulate thousands of cloud-masking scenarios in a fraction of the time. This controlled environment is what makes the 13.72% improvement possible; it gives the agent the "practice" it needs to recognize thin clouds in the "wild." Without such a framework, it would be nearly impossible to provide the agent with the millions of feedback loops required to develop a robust detection policy that works across different continents and seasons.








Chapter 3. Methodology
3.1 Research Design
This study employs a quantitative experimental research design to evaluate the effectiveness of an integrated Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework in enhancing thin cloud detection performance beyond the capabilities of traditional static Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).
The study is structured around the manipulation and measurement of the following variables:
Independent Variables (IV):
1. The Detection Methodology:
   * Control Group: Static s2cloudless CNN (Threshold fixed at 0.5).
   * Experimental Group A: s2cloudless + DQN Agent (Discrete Action Space).
   * Experimental Group B: s2cloudless + PPO Agent (Continuous Action Space).
2. Spectral Observation Inputs: Use of Cirrus Band (B10), Blue/Red Ratio, and Normalized Difference Snow Index (NDSI).
Dependent Variables (DV):
1. Detection Performance Metrics: Recall, Precision, F1-Score, and Intersection over Union (IoU), specifically targeted at measuring the leap from a 63.28% baseline to a 77.00% detection rate for thin clouds.
The research procedure is divided into three sequential phases:
Phase 1: Baseline Establishment and Environment Development
In this phase, the s2cloudless CNN is utilized to generate initial cloud probability maps. Simultaneously, a custom Gymnasium environment is developed to translate satellite imagery into a "State Space" where the AI Agent can operate.
Phase 2: Reinforcement Learning Training
The PPO and DQN agents are trained within the custom environment. The agents learn to observe spectral features and CNN outputs to decide on local threshold adjustments and confidence boosts. Training is governed by a weighted reward function:
$$Reward = 0.70 \times (Thin\_Cloud\_IoU) + 0.30 \times (F1\_Score)$$
Phase 3: Performance Evaluation and Comparative Analysis
The final phase involves testing the trained RL agents on an unseen test dataset. A comparative analysis is conducted to determine if the RL-driven dynamic thresholding significantly outperforms the static CNN baseline in "edge-case" thin cloud scenarios.

Figure 3.3.1. Proposed RL-Enhanced Cloud Detection System Pipeline.
Figure 3.3.1 illustrates the architectural design of the Proposed RL-Enhanced Cloud Detection System Pipeline, which operates as a linear, modular workflow designed to process multispectral satellite data through sequential stages of baseline estimation and intelligent refinement. 3.3.1 Pre-trained s2cloudless CNN Baseline
The foundation of the detection architecture is the s2cloudless CNN, a model specifically optimized for high-throughput cloud masking of Sentinel-2 data. The standard architecture utilizes a gradient-boosted decision tree logic internally to map spectral signatures to probability values where each pixel is assigned a score from 0 to 1.
However, traditional CNN-based masks often rely on a static global threshold (typically 0.5), which can lead to the exclusion of semi-transparent cirrus clouds. To overcome this limitation, the s2cloudless output in this study serves as the "State" for a secondary refinement layer. This allows the architecture to benefit from industry-standard accuracy while focusing computational efforts specifically on "edge-case" thin clouds. The baseline module consists of the following key components:
* Probability Mapping: Generates a 0–1 confidence layer that serves as the starting point for the refinement agent.
* Feature Preservation: A pathway that maintains the original multispectral band data to ensure the RL agent has access to raw spectral signatures during the decision-making phase.
3.3.2 Reinforcement Learning Refinement (PPO and DQN)
Following the initial probability generation, the system transitions into the decision-making phase using two distinct Reinforcement Learning architectures: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Deep Q-Network (DQN).
* Model Initialization & Training: The agents are trained within a custom Gymnasium environment designed for satellite imagery. The PPO variant is utilized for its stable, continuous policy, allowing for fine-grained threshold adjustments, while the DQN variant is tested for its efficiency in discrete, value-based actions.
* Refinement Workflow: The pipeline operates using Patch-Based Logic. High-resolution probability maps and spectral features—specifically the Cirrus Band (B10), Blue/Red Ratio, and NDSI—are processed as 64x64 pixel overlapping patches to maintain local spatial context. For each patch, the RL agent observes the environment and decides whether to lower the threshold (to increase sensitivity) or apply a thin cloud boost to elevate confidence scores.
* Comparative Evaluation: The system is designed to facilitate a comparative performance analysis. By keeping the s2cloudless input constant, the study isolates the refinement capability of each RL algorithm, determining which architecture offers the optimal balance of Precision and Recall for achieving the targeted 77.00% accuracy goal.
3.4 Data Collection and Preparation
3.4.1 Dataset Source and Generation (CloudSEN12)
While initial exploration utilized manual Sentinel-2 captures, the core of this research utilizes the CloudSEN12 Dataset, a state-of-the-art, publicly available repository specifically designed for cloud detection research. CloudSEN12 was selected due to its global diversity and its high-quality, expert-labeled annotations, which are critical for training agents to reach a 77.00% accuracy goal.
The dataset utilized in this study is characterized by the following parameters:
* Total Volume: 1,000 high-resolution image patches, ensuring sufficient feature density for deep reinforcement learning.
* Spatial Dimensions: Each patch is standardized to 512 × 512 pixels, providing a large enough context for the agent to observe cloud structures while remaining computationally efficient for training in a Google Colab environment.
* Spectral Depth: The dataset includes all 13 Sentinel-2 spectral bands, allowing the system to extract high-altitude ice signals (Band 10) and visible light scattering (Bands 2 and 4).
Data Partitioning Strategy: To ensure rigorous training and unbiased evaluation, the 1,000-image collection was organized into two strictly separated datasets:
1. Model Development Dataset (800 Images): This primary set was utilized for the iterative training loops of the PPO and DQN agents. It was further partitioned at the patch level into a Training Set (80%) for gradient updates and a Validation Set (20%) to monitor for overfitting.
2. Held-Out Test Dataset (200 Images): These images were strictly sequestered from the development phase. They serve as the definitive benchmark for the results presented in Chapter 4, ensuring that the model's performance reflects true generalization to unseen atmospheric conditions.
3.4.2 Annotation and Ground Truth Protocol
Unlike binary cloud masks, the CloudSEN12 ground truth utilizes a multi-class semantic labeling system. This allows the RL agent to specifically target "Class 2" (Thin Clouds/Cirrus) during the reward calculation phase.
3.4.3 Preprocessing and Feature Engineering Pipeline
Following dataset acquisition, a rigorous preprocessing pipeline was established using Python 3.10 and the Rasterio library. This pipeline transforms raw reflectance values into a 20-feature observation space for the agents.
A. Adaptive Image Slicing (Tessellation):
While the source patches are 512 × 512 pixels, the agents operate on a localized scale. The images were further divided into 64x64 pixel overlapping patches. This allows the agent to make "micro-decisions" regarding threshold adjustments, ensuring that small wisps of cirrus clouds are not averaged out by larger, clear-sky regions within a single frame.
B. Spectral Feature Normalization:
To ensure the stability of the PyTorch 2.0 backpropagation, all spectral features were normalized:
1. Min-Max Scaling: All 13 bands were scaled to $[0, 1]$.
2. Spectral Index Calculation: On-the-fly calculation of the Blue/Red Ratio and NDSI was performed. These indices were concatenated with the CNN’s probability output to form the agent’s final observation vector.
3. No-Data Removal: A quality control filter was applied to remove patches with missing sensor data or extreme solar glint, ensuring the agent learns from physically consistent spectral signatures.
3.4.4 Software Stack and Development Environment
The preparation and training phase leveraged a cloud-based Google Colab environment, utilizing a NVIDIA T4 GPU with 16GB of VRAM. This stack enabled persistent storage of the 73 PPO checkpoints and 10 DQN checkpoints via Google Drive integration. The use of Stable-Baselines3 ensured that the data preparation formats were natively compatible with the PPO (continuous) and DQN (discrete) environment wrappers developed in rl_thin_cloud_environment.py.
3.5 Training Procedure
The training phase for the RL-enhanced cloud detection system was conducted using a dual-algorithmic approach to compare the efficacy of continuous versus discrete decision-making. The procedure utilized a custom Gymnasium environment, where the agents learned to act as "intelligent post-processors" for the s2cloudless baseline.
3.5.1 Environment Observation and State Space
Before the training iterations began, the environment was configured to provide the agents with a 20-feature observation vector for every 64x64 pixel patch. This vector serves as the "State" ($S_t$) that the agent evaluates before taking an action. The observation includes:
* CNN Probability Stats: Mean, standard deviation, and max/min cloud probability from the s2cloudless baseline.
* Spectral Indicators: Normalized NDSI for snow/cloud discrimination, Blue/Red Ratio for scattering analysis, and Cirrus Band (B10) intensity for high-altitude ice detection.
* Spatial Context: The normalized row and column position of the patch to account for sensor-specific limb brightening or geographic variations.
3.5.2 Action Space Implementation
The primary objective of the training was for the agent to learn the optimal mapping between the spectral observations and two specific refinement actions:
1. Threshold Adjustment ($\Delta T$): Modifying the CNN’s detection cutoff to be more or less sensitive.
2. Thin Cloud Boost ($B$): Artificially increasing the confidence score in regions identified by the spectral features as semi-transparent clouds.
As detailed in the experimental setup, PPO explored this as a continuous range, allowing for infinite precision in adjustments, while DQN utilized a discrete set of 15 predefined combinations (e.g., Action 0: $-0.2$ threshold, $0.0$ boost).
3.5.3 Reward Function and Optimization Logic
To guide the agents toward the 77.00% Thin Cloud Recall goal, a multi-objective reward function was implemented. The system calculates a reward ($R_t$) for every patch processed:  
* Thin Cloud IoU: Prioritizes the recovery of pixels labeled as "Class 2" (Thin Clouds) in the CloudSEN12 ground truth.
* F1-Score: Acts as a regularizer, ensuring that while the agent "boosts" thin clouds, it does not introduce excessive False Positives (Blue areas in the visualization) that would degrade overall precision.
3.5.4 Iterative Training and Checkpointing
Training was executed on Google Colab using an NVIDIA T4 GPU, following a resumable script architecture to handle session timeouts.
* PPO Training: Executed over 720,000 steps (approximately 72 sessions). This high step count was necessary to stabilize the continuous policy gradients and ensure the agent could handle the wide variety of spectral signatures in the 800 training images.
* DQN Training: Executed over 100,000 steps (10 sessions). Due to the simpler value-based nature of DQN, the agent reached convergence significantly faster.
* Model Persistence: Every 10,000 steps, a model checkpoint was saved to Google Drive using the train_thin_cloud_resumable.py logic. This allowed for a longitudinal analysis of the learning curves and ensured that the "Best Model" (highest validation mAP) could be selected for the final test set evaluation.
3.6 Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate the effectiveness of the PPO and DQN agents in refining the s2cloudless baseline, a suite of performance metrics was utilized. While standard accuracy is reported, the primary focus of this study is on metrics that capture the model's ability to recover semi-transparent pixels without introducing excessive noise.
3.6.1 Primary Performance Metrics
The following standard metrics were calculated based on the confusion matrix components: True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN).
* Precision: Measures the exactness of the model. It defines the proportion of predicted cloud pixels that are actually clouds in the ground truth.  




   * Recall (Sensitivity): Measures the completeness of the model. It defines the proportion of actual clouds in the ground truth that were correctly identified.
  



   * F1 Score: Since increasing thin cloud detection (Recall) often leads to a decrease in Precision (false alarms), the F1 Score is utilized as the harmonic mean to provide a balanced evaluation of the system’s performance.  




3.6.2 Key Research Metric: Thin Cloud Recall
As the central goal of this research is the recovery of cirrus and semi-transparent clouds, Thin Cloud Recall is designated as the most critical performance indicator. This metric isolates "Class 2" (Thin Clouds) from the CloudSEN12 ground truth to determine exactly how much of the "hidden" atmospheric data was recovered by the RL agent.  




This metric directly measures the success of the Thin Cloud Boost action. A higher value here indicates that the agent successfully used the Cirrus Band (B10) and Blue/Red Ratio to identify features the CNN baseline missed.
3.6.3 Intersection over Union (IoU)
Intersection over Union, also known as the Jaccard Index, is used to measure the spatial overlap between the predicted cloud mask and the ground truth. This is particularly important for satellite imagery where the exact shape and boundary of the cloud are vital for downstream geospatial analysis.  




During the training phase, the Thin Cloud IoU was given a 70% weight in the reward function, forcing the agent to prioritize high-overlap accuracy for thin cloud formations.
3.6.4 Evaluation Environment
All metrics were computed on the held-out test set of 200 images (20% of the CloudSEN12 subset). By evaluating the PPO and DQN agents on data strictly sequestered from the training and validation loops, the resulting metrics represent a fair and objective measure of the system’s ability to generalize to new real-world satellite scenes.

4.1 Quantitative Performance Comparison
The quantitative evaluation focuses on the overall effectiveness of the Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents compared to the standard baseline across multiple statistical dimensions.

CHAPTER 5. Conclusion and Recommendation
5.1 Summary
The primary goal of this study was to enhance the detection of semi-transparent atmospheric features in Sentinel-2 satellite imagery by integrating a Reinforcement Learning (RL) refinement layer with the s2cloudless CNN baseline. By treating cloud masking as a sequential decision-making process, the research yielded several key findings:
      * Detection Accuracy Improvements: The RL-based refinement successfully addressed the "conservative bias" found in standard CNN models.
      * Quantitative Gains: The DQN agent achieved the highest overall performance, reaching an Accuracy of 80.9% and a superior F1-score of 78.7%.
      * Thin Cloud Recovery: The most significant breakthrough was recorded in the recovery of thin clouds, where the DQN variant achieved a Recall of 77.0%. This represents a 13.7% improvement over the CNN baseline’s recall of 63.3%.
5.1.1 Algorithm and Spectral Performance
The integration of Reinforcement Learning directly impacted the fidelity and reliability of the generated cloud masks:
      * DQN Efficiency: Despite being trained for significantly fewer steps (100k) than the PPO agent (720k), the DQN model provided more stable and accurate results, demonstrating that discrete action spaces are highly effective for pixel-wise threshold refinement.
      * Mask Fidelity: Qualitative analysis in Figure 4.3.1 confirmed that the DQN agent produced "cleaner" masks with fewer artifacts. For instance, in Patch #1, the agent recovered 82.7% of thin clouds, transforming large areas of missed detections into accurate True Positives.
      * Spectral Feature Utilization: The RL agents effectively learned to utilize specific spectral cues, such as the Cirrus (B10) band and the Blue/Red Ratio, to identify high-altitude ice crystals that were otherwise unrecognizable in the standard RGB-based probability maps.
      * Actionable Certainty: By prioritizing the Thin Cloud Recall metric, the system demonstrated an ability to recover wispy cirrus formations with high confidence, providing a more reliable atmospheric data layer for downstream climate monitoring and geospatial analysis.
5.2 Conclusion
This research successfully developed and evaluated a Reinforcement Learning-based framework for enhancing thin cloud detection in satellite imagery, addressing the critical limitation of "conservative bias" found in standard CNN architectures. Based on the experimental results and discussion, the following conclusions are drawn:
      * Superiority of RL Post-Processing: The integration of a Reinforcement Learning layer proved to be a highly effective post-processing method for refining initial cloud masks. By treating the adjustment of detection thresholds as a sequential decision-making process, the system successfully recovered atmospheric features that were otherwise omitted by the s2cloudless baseline.
      * Achievement of Thin Cloud Recovery Goals: The DQN agent emerged as the optimal model for this task, achieving the primary research objective of maximizing thin cloud recovery. With a Thin Cloud Recall of 77.0%, the model demonstrated a significant 13.7% absolute improvement over the baseline CNN, proving that RL can "see" semi-transparent features by intelligently leveraging spectral indicators.
      * Balanced Performance Metrics: Unlike traditional thresholding methods that often sacrifice precision to increase recall, the proposed RL framework maintained a robust F1-Score of 78.7%. This indicates that the system is not merely increasing the cloud mask area indiscriminately, but is making targeted, high-confidence refinements.
      * Algorithm Efficiency and Stability: The study concludes that value-based RL (DQN) is more efficient than policy-gradient methods (PPO) for pixel-wise refinement tasks. The DQN's ability to achieve superior results in only 100,000 steps compared to PPO’s 720,000 steps highlights the effectiveness of using discrete action spaces for spectral thresholding.
      * Contribution to Geospatial Analysis: By providing cleaner, more accurate cloud masks—particularly in the challenging "Class 2" thin cloud category—this research offers a more reliable data layer for downstream applications such as agricultural monitoring, climate modeling, and disaster response where clear-sky pixel availability is a primary constraint.
5.3 Recommendation
Based on the findings and limitations observed during this study, the following recommendations are proposed for future research and practical implementation of RL-based cloud masking:
      * Integration of Multi-Temporal Data: Future studies should explore the use of multi-temporal satellite sequences rather than single-scene patches. Incorporating temporal information could help the RL agent distinguish between persistent surface features and moving atmospheric clouds, potentially further reducing false positives.
      * Expansion to Heterogeneous Satellite Constellations: While this research focused on Sentinel-2, the RL framework should be tested on other sensors such as Landsat-8/9 or high-resolution commercial constellations like PlanetScope. This would verify the model's portability across different spectral resolutions and signal-to-noise ratios.
      * Physics-Informed Reward Functions: It is recommended to integrate Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) concepts into the reward function. By adding atmospheric physics constraints—such as brightness temperature or water vapor absorption models—the RL agent could achieve even higher stability in complex tropical or polar environments.
      * Optimization for Edge Computing: To support real-time disaster response, the DQN model should be optimized for deployment on edge devices or directly on board satellite hardware. Research into model quantization and pruning could allow for "on-the-fly" cloud masking before the data is even transmitted to ground stations.
      * Refinement of Action Spaces: Further experimentation with the action space granularity is suggested. Providing the agent with more specialized actions, such as "Cloud-Edge Smoothing" or "Shadow Masking," could address the minor artifacts occasionally seen in the PPO and DQN outputs.
